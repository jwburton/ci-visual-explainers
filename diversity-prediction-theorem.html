Diversity prediction theorem

Collective error = Average individual error − Diversity

(c − θ)² = 1nnΣi=1(si − θ)² − 1nnΣi=1(si − c)²

si = guess i · c = crowd estimate · θ = true value · n = crowd size

An identity (i.e., an equation that's true for every value of the variables) presented by Scott Page in The Difference (2007). It holds exactly, for any crowd, on any question.

In 1906, at the West of England Fat Stock and Poultry Exhibition in Plymouth, about 800 fairgoers each paid sixpence to guess the dressed weight of an ox, with the best guesser winning a prize. The statistician Francis Galton collected the tickets afterward. The ox weighed 1,198 pounds and the crowd's average guess was 1,197 pounds, off by just one pound and far closer to the true weight than the average guesser. The diversity prediction theorem is the algebra that shows why.

Galton, F. (1907). "Vox Populi." Nature, 75, 450–451.

Diversity always helps

Holding a crowd's average skill fixed, the theorem says a more diverse crowd is always more accurate collectively — diversity is subtracted from average error to get collective error, and a squared distance can never be negative to subtract.

It can't fix a shared blind spot

Diversity only cancels out disagreement, not a mistake everyone makes in the same direction. Push the bias slider away from zero and watch collective error refuse to fall, no matter how spread out the guesses get.
