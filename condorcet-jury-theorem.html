Condorcet’s jury theorem

A group's majority vote on a binary outcome becomes more reliable than any single member of it as the group size increases, as long as each member is at least a little better than a coin flip.

P(majority correct) = NΣk=m (Nk) pk(1−p)N−k

N = jury size · p = each juror’s independent chance of voting correctly · m = votes needed for a majority · k = number of correct votes

Nicolas de Condorcet proved this in 1785, arguing for majority rule in revolutionary France's assemblies and courts. The proof assumes every juror decides independently and is equally likely to be right every time.

Imagine a jury deciding whether a defendant is guilty. None of them knows the correct answer with certainty. Each juror is really just an informed guesser, right some percentage of the time and wrong the rest. Condorcet's question was: if you know how good each juror's judgment is on its own, how good is the jury's majority verdict?

What this theorem shows is that if every juror is even slightly better than a coin flip (i.e., independently accurate >50% of the time), a large enough jury's majority verdict is most likely correct. Accuracy climbs toward certainty as the jury grows, no matter how mediocre any single juror is. If you flip that one assumption and make individual jurors worse than a coin flip, then adding more of them makes the majority more likely to be wrong. If jurors are exactly as good as a coin flip, no jury size ever helps: the majority is just noise.

Adding jurors doesn’t make a jury smarter by itself. It amplifies whatever tendency is already there: push competence just above 50% and the majority converges towards the truth; push it just below 50% and the majority converges just as fast on being wrong.

Independence is doing all the work

The theorem needs every juror's error to be its own, uncorrelated mistake. But in reality, jurors talk, defer to confident voices, and share the same news coverage and biases. Correlated errors don't cancel out the way independent ones do, no matter how many jurors you add (Ladha, 1992).
