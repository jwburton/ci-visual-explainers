Diversity trumps ability

A random team of varied problem-solvers can out-search a team of the individually best problem-solvers.

Hong and Page (2004) show — through computer simulations — that this is a probabilistic tendency that holds under specific conditions: the problem has to be hard, the pool of possible problem-solvers has to be large and diverse, and the individuals need to be reasonably competent to begin with. Simulate some trials below and watch how often it holds.

How this demo differs from Hong and Page (2004)

What matches the study. The random team is drawn from the whole pool of pizza makers, with the best ones left in, so a top maker can still land on the random team by luck. Both teams work the same way: members take turns, and anyone who can find a topping change that improves the pizza makes it, until no one on the team can do any better. A maker’s skill is measured by how good a pizza they tend to reach on their own, tried from many different random starting pizzas.

What is simplified. The pizza kitchen stands in for the kind of genuinely hard problem the study used (a standard one from the research literature; Kauffman & Weinberger, 1989). Ours is deliberately small so it runs instantly: 16 toppings, 60 makers to choose from, and teams of 3 to 8, where the study used far larger numbers. Here each maker simply has a fixed set of toppings they are allowed to change; in the study, people also differ in how they size up the problem and which changes they try, not just which parts they can touch. And the win rate is a running tally over many repeated trials rather than a single contest: the best team stays fixed for a given landscape (kitchen), while the random team is reshuffled each trial.

Imagine you are making a pizza with 16 possible toppings, and for each one you decide: add the topping or leave the topping off. A topping's contribution to the pizza's overall taste doesn't stand alone; it depends on which other toppings are also on the pizza. Pineapple with ham might taste great, but pineapple with anchovies, less so. Whether the toppings' contributions are sensititve to the presence of other toppings depends on K. When K is small, a topping's flavor barely depends on the rest of the pizza, so good combinations are easier to find — just add the toppings that independently taste good and the pizza keeps getting better, like walking up a single smooth hill. When K is large, almost every topping's flavor swings depending on the rest of the combination, and the solutuion space fills with false peaks. For instance, you might start making a very good pizza, but then swapping just one topping makes it inedible.

Each problem-solver (pizza maker) below has a few of the 16 possible toppings to work with (controlled by the m slider), but no one has time to taste-test every possible pizza. A team pools whatever its members can each try: at every step, whoever can find a swap (i.e., add a topping) that improves the pizza makes it, until nobody can improve it any further.

Kauffman, S. A., & Weinberger, E. D. (1989). The NK model of rugged fitness landscapes and its application to maturation of the immune response. Journal of Theoretical Biology, 141(2), 211–245.

Ability doesn’t add up the way you’d think

Picking the best individuals doesn’t maximize what the group can collectively see, because the traits (toppings at one's disposal) that make someone an individually good problem-solver (pizza maker) tend to make them good for similar reasons, and similar reasons mean overlapping toolboxes.

This is a tendency, not a guarantee

Hong and Page's result needs a hard enough problem, a large and varied pool to draw from, and individuals who are competent enough to begin with (notice that the lowest solo ability is > 50). Push perspective size (m) up toward the whole problem and the advantage shrinks, because everyone starts to see everything anyway (i.e., everyone has access to all toppings).
